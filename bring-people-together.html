Project 2

Bring People
Together

Design something that gets real people working in a product together, and test it with at least one hundred unique people.

This project is adapted from Kelin Zhang's “Bring People Together,” written for her AI Software Design Studio at RISD.

Let's design an experience that brings people together, digitally (and maybe physically).

For this project, design a way to bring people together, definitely online and possibly ALSO in real life. This can be anything you can imagine that gets people working collaboratively (or maybe adversarially?): a game, a puzzle, a protest, a job, a date, a fistfight.

We're going to use new technology, specifically AI-powered coding tools and shared libraries that allow multiplayer interactions to bring people together. You're going to design something new but you're also going to deploy it where people can use your real software.

The core tech idea here is a synchronous multiplayer mode: designing an interface that supports many people using it simultaneously.

Client, groups, goals

Your client

Real people; but which ones?

Many class projects design for imaginary users. We're going to talk with real people, show them real prototypes, and see what they actually do.

You'll choose a group of people, but it can be anything - a specific interest group or just whomever you can get to test-drive your thing. We'll start with motivations and then generate ideas.

Goals
  • Design for real people, not imaginary users. Be comfortable talking to strangers, showing them unfinished prototypes, and gathering feedback.
  • Design for multiple people at once. Multiplayer is/will be the default for many kinds of software. Let's be thinking about how to use it. How do we show users that 'others are here'? How do we show users that 'others are watching'?
  • Aim at behavior as well as the artifact. The thing we're making is only good if it supports activity between and amongst people.
  • Get comfortable with scrappy, fast, ugly, iterative prototyping.
  • Think about distribution and network effects. How do you get real people to use something? WHY do they want to use your thing?

Requirements

  • 100 testers. That's a minimum. We're going for multiplayer interaction and we want to see a LOT of it.
  • Get at least 5 people into live interaction with each other through the final version of your project. Five at the same time, not five over the course of a week. Anywhere this brief says 5 people, that is what it means. Live can mean software (a chat, a call, a shared canvas) but can also include real life (like a synchronous game, a scavenger hunt, a meetup, etc). If you've got an in-real-life component the users must also interact with your software.
  • Document those interactions. Video, screen recordings, chat logs, photos. Whatever helps tell your story.
  • There must be a software component. It doesn't have to be a phone app, though: a chatbot? a website? an SMS / imessage? a bot in a Discord?
  • Multiplayer interaction. Whatever you make, it's got to support 5 people using it at once, with synchronous activity and visibility.
  • Test with real people every single week. If you can't get a prototype testable in a week, talk to me about scoping down. Every week! No exceptions.
  • Think about safety. Add basic guardrails against destructive behavior. We'll deal with this in specific class sessions.

Your group repo

Every group keeps a shared repo on GitHub, and all members of the team commit to it. Now you know how to use github, and branches, and markdown!

Your github repo will be for code, eventually. But it's also for writing and coordination.

  • README.md -> what your project is in plain language. You should rewrite this every time the project changes.
  • log.md —> a running log that's updated AT LEAST weekly. What did we test, with whom, what happened, what surprised us. This is like a work journal!
  • todo.md —> keep a shared to-do list, which you can assign names and due dates.
  • notes/ —> That's a folder with one file per interview. You don't need to make these polished, just capture what happened.
  • numbers.md —> your running tally toward 100 people reached (minimum!) and 5 in live interaction. Update it every week.

Rules

  • Everybody commits to the repo. Remember we can all see your commit histories! Everybody's work should show up in the repo.
  • Write in markdown. Your logs, todos, notes, etc should be in plain text, readable by a human (and also readable by an LLM).
  • Commit when something happens, not once at the end of the week.

Deliverables & constraints

Deliverables
  1. A working piece of software.
  2. Your group repo, with a real commit history from all of you. README, weekly log, todos, interview notes, crit notes, running numbers. Also this is where your project code goes! Make it public so we can snoop and learn from each other.
  3. A group Figma file for the visual design work: storyboards, mockups, screens, test documentation. Words and code go in the repo.
  4. Evidence you hit the numbers. 100 people reached, and 5 or more in live interaction at the same time.
  5. A final presentation, 20 minutes. Use what you practiced in your Hear Me Out presentations!
Constraints
  • Five weeks. User tests every week.
  • You may use AI to help build it. Your AI-usage doesn't need to be user-facing, but it can be.
  • It doesn't need to be an app. In fact, ideas catch on more easily when they ride on behavior people already have, instead of asking them to learn a new one like learning new software or downloading a new app.
  • Words, notes, todos, and numbers live in the repo. Storyboards, mocks, and screens live in Figma. Your presentation is yours to build in whatever you want.

How to get started

What common motivators could you tap into?

Finding love. Making money. Hunger. Thirst. Fear. Anger. Convenience. Fun. Mystery and novelty. Exclusivity and status. Achievement and validation. The seven deadly sins (counterpoint: the seven virtues). Mismatches in what people need, eg people who want tutoring and people who want to tutor.

What groups do you actually have access to?

Your family, and families like yours. Specific clubs and orgs at KU. Students at other schools. Grad students if you're an undergrad. Recent grads. The people who work at the places you go every week. Your dorm, your floor, your building. Fandoms you're already in. Groups you already belong to could work.

What is the magic circle in your idea?

What are the rules, and where do they stop applying?

Mechanics worth stealing

Games and competitions. A daily repeating event (Wordle, BeReal). Anything with a mystery and a reveal. A marketplace between people who have something and people who need it.

How do you find more people?

Posting in Discords and online communities. Going viral, if you're lucky. Asking existing users to bring you new ones. (This one actually works!)

Sessions

Week 1 of 5. Get out there and talk to people!

Session I

Kickoff

  • Project brief. Groups assigned.
  • Open the same tldraw room, all of us at once. Draw on each other's work for a few minutes. Then: how did you know somebody else was there? What happened when two of you grabbed the same thing?
  • Discussion: other multiplayer modes you have seen or used. What did they get right?
  • Discussion: what does it mean to design for behavior as well as an artifact?
  • Discussion: how will you bring people together?
  • Meet your group. Decide how you're splitting the work, and write it down.
  • Set up your group repo before you leave the room tonight. One person creates it, everyone else gets added, everyone clones it. Every name in the group should show up in the commit log before you leave.
  • Plan your interviews: who, when, and what you actually want to find out. Remember from CCC: "what do you think?" is not a research question.
Homework

Write your first README.md. One paragraph about who you might be designing for and why.

And homework

Each person talks to 3 people. In person or on a call but no surveys. 30 to 60 minutes each, so there's time to get past small talk. Record audio; use AI transcription and an LLM to help you pull out takeaways.

Ask about the group activities and experiences they're already part of: who they are, what their days are like, how they want to connect with people, and what's getting in the way.

Post the interview notes to your repo

And homework

Use and document three different multiplayer things. A game, a shared document, a chat, etc. Write down what designed choices told you other people were in there with you. Bring the list to discuss Wednesday.

Session II

What did you hear? / Spitball some ideas

  • Group readouts from the interviews. What surprised you? Your notes should already be committed.
  • Cluster the people you talked to. Is it a demographic? A shared trait? A shared frustration? Can you pick one kind of person to aim at for now?
  • Compare/contrast interaction points from the multiplayer tests. What common tools do we have?
In Class

10 ideas each (so 30 or 40 as a group), each one a 2–4 panel storyboard comic. Can you pull from the interviews? There needs to be a software component but it does not need to be an app. Sharpies & paper. Pin it up on the wall.

And homework

As a group, pick 3-4 concepts (one per person) and prototype each of them. Test each with 5 or more people. These early tests can be one person at a time if you need to. Prototypes can be Figma mocks, a text bot, a site Claude built you in an hour, or a wizard-of-oz simulation (eg you texting people manually and pretending it's automated). Don't bother with a visually polished prototype yet! Document reactions.

Ask yourself: did they do what you expected? What surprised you? What flopped?

Document the prototypes and user reactions in your repo.

Week 2 of 5. Iterate & test

Session III

Crit #1

  • Every group presents: your prototypes, your testing.
  • Present from your repo. Open the log and the numbers and show us what you did, including your notes in your repo. Every crit should work this way now.
  • We'll workshop scope. Does your project need to be bigger or smaller?
  • If nothing from week one worked, you can pivot to new ideas!
  • As a group: is there a winner? Can you lock in on one of these concepts? Ideally you can decide by next class.
  • Before next class: commit your crit notes and rewrite your README as appropriate.
Homework

Before Wednesday, get a multiplayer proof-of-concept demo running. I think you should choose tldraw sync or PartyKit, follow their quickstart, and deploy something that multiple people can use at once. It does not have to be actually relevant to your project. Come with that working so we spend class on your project instead of setup.

Session IV

Safety & Community

  • What did you settle on as a group? What are you going to build?
  • Discussion: What does a GOOD community use of this look like? How can you plan for and support it?
  • Discussion: how would an angry ex use this?
  • Guardrails, reporting, blocking, rate limits. What is the least you can build that keeps people reasonably safe?
Homework

Get a working multiplayer version of your own project deployed, even if it barely does anything. Then test it with 15+ new people, at least 3 sessions of 5+ people at a time. Document it! Video? Screen record? etc. Notes go into your repo. Keep your log up to date. Divide up the work, use your todo (or even the issues function) to share. Create a threats.md file per Wednesday's discussion. What are you going to protect against, what are you going to allow as a trade-off?
